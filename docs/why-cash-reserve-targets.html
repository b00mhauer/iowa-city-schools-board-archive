Why a 60–90 day cash reserve matters

The General Fund's cash balance swings by about 50 days of cash within every year — not because the district gains or loses money, but because property taxes and state aid arrive in a few big lumps while payroll goes out evenly. A reserve target has to be sized to the seasonal low point, not the average. That is why a healthy target is 60–90 days, even though the year-round average looks higher.

The problem: cash is highly seasonal

Local property taxes land in just two months (October & April); state aid is steady; payroll never pauses. So cash drains between tax settlements and bottoms out in late summer before October taxes arrive. The red line is what ICCSD actually looked like in FY26 without its rescue borrowing — the trough goes negative. Lift the whole curve to a 60- or 90-day reserve and the same trough clears the floor with no borrowing.

Why the average is the wrong number. A district could average a comfortable-sounding 35 days and still go broke every August. What matters is the trough — the single lowest point — relative to the cash you must keep on hand to make payroll. The reserve exists to carry the trough, not the average.
New for FY27 — a state law reshapes the season, but not the target. Iowa SF 2201 (signed Feb 2026) moves state aid from monthly to quarterly payments beginning July 15. That refills the fund at the start of the year, so the late-summer trough above eases — but the dry stretch simply moves to late winter (Dec–March), between quarterly payments. Because four big payments are lumpier than nine small ones, the year-to-year swing actually grows, so the 60–90 day target holds — arguably reinforced. The seasonality reshapes; it does not shrink.

Why 60–90 days, specifically

The target is built from three pieces:

  • Operating floor (~20 days) — cash you can never dip below, because payroll is paid mid-month before late-month receipts arrive.
  • Seasonal dip (~27 days) — how far the late-summer trough sits below the annual average, baked in by the tax calendar.
  • Volatility / shock buffer (~13 → 43 days) — margin for a year where revenues come in light or costs run heavy. A modest buffer gets you to 60; covering a real shock (enrollment drop, delayed aid) takes you toward 90.

A second, independent check agrees: modeled month by month, the demanding months (the post-tax peaks, which must self-fund the entire run down to the next trough) require 60–76 days for 95–99% confidence of never running dry. A single reserve policy must satisfy the most demanding month — so the floor lands at ~60, the prudent level near ~90.

What each month needs

Required days of cash for a 95% chance of not running dry over the following 12 months (component-based volatility model). September is the universal binding trough; the peaks need the most cushion because the whole drawdown is still ahead of them.

Bottom line for ICCSD

The district currently steers to roughly 20–35 days — below even the minimum — which is exactly why it has borrowed every late summer (the $10M interfund loan, the anticipatory warrants). Those are the symptom of a reserve sized to the average instead of the trough. Moving the floor toward 60 days, and the goal toward 90, would let the General Fund self-fund its own seasonal cycle, end the recurring summer borrowing and its interest cost, and rebuild the margin that rating agencies look for.
